SERIOUS GAMES AND SIMULATION WORKSHOPS AT DIFFERENT STAGES OF IT SPECIALIST TRAINING

Abstract. The article investigates the scientific and methodological foundations, instructional design, and practical outcomes of implementing differentiated models of serious games and simulation workshops in higher education for training information technology and project management specialists. The relevance of the study stems from the urgent need to bridge the gap between academic instruction and dynamic industry demands under conditions of digitalization and blended learning. The paper substantiates tailoring game mechanics to students' educational level and professional maturity. Two autonomous instructional models were designed and empirically validated: a scenario-based learning (SBL) workshop on the Mural virtual collaborative whiteboard for second-cycle (master's) students (N = 16) and a team-based Scrum development simulation utilizing GitHub Projects for first-cycle (undergraduate) students (N = 27). In the master's cohort, a five-phase role-playing simulation on preventing academic dishonesty was conducted using a constrained voting resource (8 tokens), which stimulated a transition from divergent brainstorming to the rational selection of 14 project proposals. In the undergraduate cohort, three subgroups simulated team collaboration on the VARTA training project across two learning cycles, delivering an end-to-end working prototype and coordinating technical interfaces across subgroups. Based on GitHub platform records, group dynamics were analyzed in terms of task structure, complexity estimation, and completion rates. The study identified an early-success overconfidence phenomenon: in the second cycle, students independently inflated their planned workload by 50% (from 44 to 60 points against a 40-point norm), triggering a controlled time-planning crisis with 23.2% of tasks left unfinished. Experiencing this instructional situation fostered self-regulation, realistic planning, and accountability. Methodological recommendations for university instructors on designing and facilitating simulation workshops are formulated.

Keywords: serious games, simulation workshops, scenario-based learning, Scrum, gamification, decision-making, GitHub Projects, project management, higher education.

Introduction. Higher education in Ukraine is undergoing a transition from predominantly reproductive knowledge transmission toward interactive and practice-oriented instructional formats. Amid the widespread adoption of blended and distance learning modalities, developing the professional and digital competencies of future information technology specialists has become especially crucial [1]. Providing high-quality training for specialists in software engineering and project management requires a differentiated pedagogical approach that accounts for students' academic degree level and professional maturity.

For first-cycle (bachelor's) students (specifically second-year undergraduates), who are just beginning to master collaborative software engineering, the primary hurdle is bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge of programming and algorithms and the practical creation of a shared software product. At this foundational stage, fostering essential skills in team interaction, role allocation, and the practical application of agile methodologies (Scrum) using modern version control and project planning tools is paramount. Conversely, for second-cycle (master's) students, who already possess baseline technical training and are advancing toward analytical and managerial roles, the priority shifts toward strategic planning, resolving ethical dilemmas, and decision-making under uncertainty and resource scarcity.

One of the most effective pedagogical frameworks for addressing these objectives is the methodology of serious games [2, 3] and Scenario-Based Learning (SBL) [4]. A well-structured and psychologically safe simulation environment empowers undergraduates to practice collaborative technical workflows and master's students to make complex managerial decisions without exposing stakeholders to financial or reputational risks.

Literature Review. The theoretical and methodological underpinnings of gamification and serious games in education are extensively established in the seminal works of K. Kapp [2], who examined the adaptation of game elements across different educational tiers. The significance of play in stimulating divergent thinking and cognitive development was explored by J. Brown [5]. The MDA (Mechanics-Dynamics-Aesthetics) framework, formalized by R. Hunicke, M. LeBlanc, and R. Zubek [6], provides a conceptual foundation for educational game design by explicating the relationship among formal rules (mechanics), run-time participant behavior (dynamics), and emotional/cognitive reception (aesthetics). Approaches to evaluating the effectiveness of serious games in software engineering education were systematically categorized by A. Calderón and M. Ruiz [7].

The integration of agile frameworks—most notably Scrum—into higher education curricula has been actively investigated by Ukrainian and international scholars. V. Varenyk and Zh. Piskova [8] analyzed the implementation of Scrum within Ukrainian universities to foster communicative competencies. The modeling of communication workflows and collective decision-making in Scrum environments was analyzed by O. Kichmarenko, Ye. Platonova, and V. Platonov [9]. A. V. Chorna substantiated the efficacy of Scrum as an end-to-end practical training vehicle for software engineers [10]. The affordances of digital game technologies in structuring blended learning environments in higher education were comprehensively examined by N. V. Morze and M. A. Gladun [11].

At the international level, broad systematic reviews of empirical studies on agile adoption were synthesized by T. Dybå and T. Dingsøyr [12]. Curricular frameworks for teaching agile software engineering in university settings were proposed by H. Sharif and C. Abran [13], as well as by M. Kropp and A. Meier [14], who emphasized the critical necessity of coupling technical assignments with authentic instructional simulations. Nevertheless, the academic literature lacks sufficient comparative analysis and multi-level differentiation of game-based simulations across the educational continuum—ranging from conceptual-analytical scenario workshops in graduate education to tool-centered engineering simulations in undergraduate programs.

Research Goal and Objectives. The aim of this research is to theoretically substantiate, design, and comparatively evaluate two differentiated simulation learning models in higher IT education: a conceptual scenario-based workshop for master's students and a collaborative Scrum simulation for undergraduate students.

To achieve this goal, the following research objectives were addressed:
1. Analyze approaches to selecting game mechanics based on the educational level and professional maturity of students.
2. Design and empirically test a scenario-based learning (SBL) workshop model utilizing resource scarcity mechanics for master's students.
3. Architect and execute a collaborative simulation of the full software development lifecycle grounded in Scrum and GitHub Projects for undergraduate students.
4. Analyze empirical quantitative and qualitative indicators of group dynamics (task volume and complexity, risks of workload overestimation, and the role of technical infrastructure), and formulate methodological recommendations for university instructors.

Materials and Methods. The study employed pedagogical observation and the analysis of instructional activity data conducted throughout the 2023–2024 academic year at the Department of Optimal Control and Economic Cybernetics of Odesa I. I. Mechnikov National University.

Two student cohorts participated in the study:
1. Master's cohort (Year 1 of the master's program / second educational cycle, N = 16 students enrolled in Major 111 "Mathematics" and Major 113 "Applied Mathematics") within the course "Project and Startup Management." The workshop spanned 4 academic hours and took place in the virtual collaborative workspace of the Mural platform [15].
2. Undergraduate cohort (Year 2 of the bachelor's program / first educational cycle, N = 27 students enrolled in Major 113 "Applied Mathematics") within the course "Software Product Development and Testing Technologies." The practicum spanned 4 laboratory sessions (8 academic hours) structured as two sequential Scrum cycles. Project planning, role distribution, and task tracking were conducted on GitHub Projects digital boards.

The research methodology incorporated:
- Scenario engineering and instructional game modeling (designing project lore, interaction protocols, and evaluation rubrics);
- Analysis of digital learning activity metadata on GitHub (task counts and classifications, complexity estimates, completion rates, and update frequencies);
- Qualitative artifact content analysis (virtual sticky notes on Mural, decision diagrams, and retrospective student reflections);
- Structured pedagogical observation and reflective analysis of group dynamics.

Results. The designed simulation models differ in the complexity of their managerial and technical challenges in alignment with students' educational tiers.

1. Scenario-Based Learning Workshop for Master's Students (Mural Platform)
For graduate students, a conceptual workshop was instituted to cultivate competencies in strategic planning, strict resource prioritization, and ethical conflict resolution. The authentic challenge posed to the master's students was to engineer a comprehensive institutional framework for preventing academic dishonesty among lower-division undergraduates. Master's students assumed the role of expert consultants, leveraging their own educational background, which heightened their engagement and personal accountability for decisions.

The workshop was structured around the Scenario-Based Learning (SBL) model [4] across five sequential phases, each serving a distinct didactic function:
- Phase 1: Immersion. The instructor acted as the client formulating a strategic challenge: establishing an effective academic integrity support ecosystem. The master's students approached the situation not as passive subjects of control, but as solution architects, fostering systems thinking and executive responsibility.
- Phase 2: Exploration. Over a 10-minute window, participants conducted a divergent brainstorming sprint on a Mural board, generating maximum proposals on colored sticky notes without prior critique. The brisk pace and synchronous visualization removed fear of failure, stimulated divergent thinking, and pooled individual ideas into a shared group intellectual asset.
- Phase 3: Convergence (Idea Clustering). Participants organized sticky notes into thematic clusters, eliminated redundancies, and established interconnections between proposals. This cultivated analytical and critical thinking through collaborative evaluation of idea quality and viability.
- Phase 4: Choice Crisis. A resource scarcity game mechanic was applied: each participant received exactly 8 votes (tokens) to support top initiatives. This artificial constraint mirrored budget or time limits in real projects, compelling students to transition from uncritical broad endorsement to rational selection guided by the MDA framework [6].
- Phase 5: Consequence Analysis. Prioritized initiatives were plotted onto an Action Priority Matrix ("Feasibility vs. Importance"), while unsupported proposals fell into the "dead zone." Analyzing this zone illuminated the trade-offs inherent in managerial compromises, assessed idea feasibility, and trained professional argumentation in defending choices.

This structural sequence transformed the session into a cohesive model of real-world project work where graduate students practiced managerial and communicative competencies in a safe instructional environment.

2. Teamwork Simulation Workshop for Undergraduates (GitHub Projects Environment)
For second-year undergraduates, a software engineering Scrum simulation was deployed. The student cohort was divided into three parallel subgroups (Alpha, Beta, Gamma) of 9 members each. Each subgroup allocated core project team roles: systems analyst, UI/UX designer, software developers, QA engineers, and infrastructure/DevOps engineers. To understand the full product lifecycle, students could rotate roles between stages. Instructors served as the Product Owner and Scrum Master, providing feedback and facilitation without direct authoritarian intervention.

The conceptual core of the practicum was VARTA—a decentralized municipal resource coordination platform designed for civil emergencies. Moving away from boilerplate CRUD assignments was didactically significant: an unconventional domain compelled students toward systems analysis and cross-subgroup communication. The work was structured into two sequential cycles on GitHub Projects Kanban boards with explicit task categorization (requirements analysis, UI design, coding, testing, technical infrastructure), instilling disciplined incremental planning.

In Cycle 1, teams concentrated on producing an end-to-end working prototype that integrated analysis, user interface, backend logic, and basic verification into a deployable result. In Cycle 2, the focus shifted toward establishing technical interface contracts between subgroups, subsystem integration, and remediating accumulated infrastructure debt. 3. Pedagogical Analysis of Group Dynamics Indicators and Learning Activity Structure
To evaluate undergraduate workflow behavior objectively, quantitative metrics were gathered across both learning cycles for the three subgroups (Table 2). The nominal planned capacity for each subgroup per cycle was calibrated at 40 Story Points (SP) (totaling 120 SP across all three teams).

Analysis of learning activity distribution demonstrated a pronounced evolution in development focus between the two cycles:
1. In Cycle 1, students focused predominantly on preparatory and analytical workflows. Quality assurance and testing constituted the largest share (18.8%), followed by general technical engineering tasks (17.2%), and UI/UX interface design (14.8%). This reflects lower-division undergraduates' initial caution when approaching a complex domain and their desire to organize requirements prior to coding. Activity distribution across subgroups was uneven: Alpha generated 48.4% of tasks, while Beta and Gamma generated 26.6% and 25.0%, respectively. This asymmetry indicates the natural emergence of a lead subgroup and highlights the need for pedagogical support for less confident teams.
2. In Cycle 2, team focus shifted squarely toward implementation and inter-subgroup coordination. Component engineering (24.2%) and shared technical infrastructure / cross-team coordination (23.2%) became prominent. Compared to Cycle 1, the share of infrastructure and coordination tasks increased by 64.0%. Team activity was distributed as follows: Alpha – 54.5%, Beta – 10.1%, Gamma – 35.4%. Team Alpha's heavy workload resulted from voluntarily assuming the role of central coordination hub between project subsystems.

A salient pedagogical finding was the emergence of an "initial success illusion." Emboldened by completing Cycle 1 (44 SP achieved against a 40 SP baseline), students became overly optimistic and independently scheduled 60 SP for Cycle 2 (a 50% commitment surge). Underestimating the complexity of cross-subgroup coordination resulted in missed deadlines: 23.2% of tasks remained unfinished. However, this controlled crisis held substantial pedagogical value: by directly experiencing the consequences of overpromising, students recognized the vital necessity of realistic workload estimation, task decomposition, and proactive communication.

Discussion. Comparing the outcomes of both simulation workshops reveals several key patterns in organizing simulation-based learning:

1. Differences in Perception of Structure and Abstraction Level. Master's students demonstrated readiness to navigate open-ended environments on Mural, translating an abstract integrity challenge into 14 viable project initiatives. In contrast, second-year undergraduates required concrete operational boundaries and the structured workflow of GitHub Projects with predefined issue templates and progress stages. This corroborates conclusions by M. Kropp and A. Meier [14] as well as findings by S. O. Semerikov et al. [1] regarding junior students' reliance on explicit scaffolding when acquiring digital and project competencies.

2. Overestimation of Learning Capabilities and the Planning Crisis. The most telling observation in the undergraduate cohort was the controlled planning crisis during Cycle 2. Following early success, students overestimated their capacity, scheduling 60 SP against a 40 SP baseline (+50%), which left 23.2% of tasks incomplete due to cross-module integration bottlenecks. Experiencing this controlled failure yielded a far deeper learning effect than traditional verbal warnings: students experienced firsthand the critical importance of disciplined planning and prior interface agreement. Similar self-organization dynamics in educational team projects have been reported by T. Dybå and T. Dingsøyr [12] and by V. Varenyk and Zh. Piskova [8].

3. Significance of Resource Constraints and Timely Technical Preparation. In the graduate cohort, rationed voting tokens (8 per participant) under the MDA model [6] prevented superficial consensus and filtered out unfeasible ideas. In the undergraduate simulation, early operational (Ops) execution proved critical: attempting feature implementation without foundational infrastructure caused significant assembly delays, affirming the need to introduce infrastructure tasks from early sessions.

Scientific Novelty of the Study lies in theoretically substantiating and empirically verifying an MDA-grounded framework for differentiating game simulations according to students' educational tiers (scarcity-driven decision-making for graduate students vs. regulated collaborative development for undergraduates), as well as identifying and modeling the mechanism of cognitive workload overestimation following initial success.

Practical Significance of the findings consists in providing higher education instructors with ready-to-use pedagogical toolkits (a Mural scenario workshop blueprint, a competency matrix, and a GitHub Projects evaluation framework) adaptable across diverse computing and management curricula.

Limitations of the Study. The study's generalizability is subject to limitations: it was conducted within a single university (Odesa I. I. Mechnikov National University), and the undergraduate simulation was bounded by two academic cycles within one semester. Future investigations should validate these findings across broader student samples and diverse institutional settings.

Ethical Standards Compliance. The study adhered strictly to pedagogical research ethics and academic integrity principles. All participants were informed of instructional session recordings and gave voluntary consent; video and platform data were stored under restricted access exclusively for research purposes and analyzed anonymously. Generative AI tools were employed solely for linguistic editing and translation without modifying scholarly content.

Conclusions. Implementing serious games and simulation workshops in higher education requires intentional differentiation aligned with students' academic degree levels:
1. For master's students, scenario-based learning workshops (such as on Mural) are optimal; their five-phase architecture—from problem immersion to dead-zone analysis—facilitates a deliberate transition from open brainstorming to rigorous strategic selection.
2. For undergraduate students, structured collaborative engineering simulations (such as Scrum in GitHub Projects) are most effective, establishing baseline skills in task decomposition, team interaction, and engineering discipline.

This study theoretically grounded and validated a two-tiered game mechanics differentiation model based on the MDA framework aligned with students' professional maturity; refined agile education methodologies through experiencing a controlled planning crisis and prior inter-subgroup contract negotiation; and clarified approaches for identifying and resolving infrastructure debt using digital project boards.

Based on the study, the following methodological recommendations are formulated for university instructors:
1. Allocate dedicated instructional time for collaborative backlog refinement prior to each cycle, coaching students in relative estimation and granular task decomposition.
2. Provide calibrated pedagogical scaffolding: grant teams measured autonomy during initial stages, deliberately permitting common planning pitfalls to surface, followed by a structured post-cycle retrospective to debrief root causes.
3. Embed engineering standards directly into evaluation rubrics (e.g., conditioning feature implementation on prior inter-subgroup API contract approval).
4. Support infrastructure workflows from the initial session, providing students with standardized repository boilerplate templates to prevent downstream technical bottlenecks.
5. In scenario workshops, strictly delineate open ideation and convergent selection phases, using voting resource scarcity as a didactic catalyst for evidence-based prioritization.

Future Research Directions include examining the impact of cross-subgroup role rotation on skill acquisition speed among undergraduates, conducting longitudinal tracking of simulation-based training on graduate employability, and developing transparent peer-assessment rubrics for evaluating collaborative soft skills.
